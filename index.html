Microwave Magnonics Lab

We study spin waves, or magnons: ripples in the magnetization of a material that can carry information without moving any electric charge. We look at how they talk to sound, microwaves and superconducting circuits, and how to build useful devices from them.

Research

Magnons, the quanta of spin waves, carry information at gigahertz frequencies without moving electric charge. Their frequency tunes with a magnetic field, and they couple strongly to lattice vibrations (phonons) and to microwave photons.

Most of our work happens where these three meet. We use phonons to couple magnets that are far apart, connect magnons to superconducting circuits to pass quantum information between platforms, and use nonlinear spin waves for signal processing.

  • Magnon–phonon coupling

    In magnetic garnets, magnons and sound waves (phonons) mix strongly. We use phonons to carry angular momentum coherently between magnets that are far apart, and to make two magnets behave as one coupled system with bright and dark states.

    • Phys. Rev. X 12, 011060 (2022)
    • Phys. Rev. B 101, 060407(R) (2020)
    • Phys. Rev. Applied 20, 014046 (2023)

  • Quantum magnonics

    Magnons also couple to microwave photons and superconducting qubits, which makes them good candidates for quantum transducers and sensors. In our Open-Lab project we are building a cryogenic setup where magnons meet superconducting circuits, including experiments on squeezed magnon states.

    • Phys. Rev. X 12, 011060 (2022)

  • Spin-wave signal processing

    Spin waves carry signals without charge currents, and when they get strong they start to interact, which can be used for logic and brain-inspired computing. We look at these nonlinear effects with very fine frequency resolution and design antennas that send spin waves one way but not the other.

    • Nat. Commun. 15, 7302 (2024)
    • J. Korean Phys. Soc. 88, 1094 (2026)
    • Phys. Rev. Applied 11, 034065 (2019)

  • Spin caloritronics

    Heat travels through magnetic insulators as both magnons and phonons. We measure how the two fall out of step and how hot magnons spread, as groundwork for energy-efficient spin devices.

    • Phys. Rev. B 103, 174432 (2021)
    • Phys. Rev. Lett. 117, 107202 (2016)

Setups

  • FMR setup

    A magnetic film sits on between the poles of our electromagnet. As we sweep the field, the film suddenly absorbs microwaves when its spins precess in step with them. That is ferromagnetic resonance, the same measurement as the game at the top of this page.

    • Electromagnet with a bipolar power supply
    • we make ourselves
    • Rotation stage run by

  • Spin-wave setup

    Here antennas launch spin waves that run along the film. Spin waves of this kind (Damon–Eshbach waves) travel better in one direction than the other, and our first paper shows how the antenna design changes that.

    • Antennas for Damon–Eshbach spin waves
    • Transmission spectra from the network analyser

  • Microwave instruments

    One rack holds the electronics that the FMR and spin-wave setups share. The handwritten note on it says “measuring”, which is how we tell each other not to touch anything.

    • Agilent four-port vector network analyser
    • Rohde & Schwarz microwave signal generator
    • Agilent spectrum analyser
    • Stanford Research lock-in amplifiers, one of them for radio frequencies

  • MOKE setup

    Polarised laser light that reflects off a magnet comes back slightly rotated, and the rotation follows the magnetisation. With this magneto-optical Kerr effect (MOKE) we watch how a magnet switches as we sweep the field.

    • Cobolt laser
    • Polarisers and Thorlabs optics

  • Kerr microscope

    The same effect seen through a microscope: polarised light, a 10× objective and a camera let us look at magnetic domains directly. Our undergraduates used it to see how hitting a steel rod changes its magnetisation.

    • Mitutoyo 10× objective
    • Thorlabs cage optics with a polariser in a rotation mount
    • Camera on top of the column

  • Microwave diffraction setup

    Crystals you can hold: steel balls on 3D-printed layers make simple cubic, BCC and FCC lattices, and microwaves play the part of X-rays. Our undergraduates measured Bragg diffraction with it for the KPS meeting.

    • 3D-printed lattices with steel balls
    • Two antennas on Thorlabs stands around a turntable
    • Portable network analyser

Kyongmo An

Assistant Professor, Department of Physics Education, Chungbuk National University

Kyongmo started M² Lab in March 2025. Much of his research is about spin waves meeting sound and microwaves: Brillouin light scattering of magnons and phonons during his Ph.D. at UT Austin, spin-wave devices at EPFL, phonon-coupled magnets at SPINTEC in Grenoble, and hybrid magnonics at KRISS.

News

  1. October 2026

    Two undergraduate teams at the KPS Fall Meeting, Jeju

    Two teams of our undergraduates presented their projects in the undergraduate physics research competition at the Korean Physical Society Fall Meeting. One team showed how hitting a steel rod over and over shifts its magnetization toward the Earth's magnetic field, measured through the rod's stray field and with MOKE imaging. The other team built simple cubic, BCC and FCC crystals by moving steel balls on a 3D-printed lattice and measured their Bragg diffraction with microwaves and a portable network analyser.

  2. October 2026
